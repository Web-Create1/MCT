1. Executive Summary

This brief examines the complete technical pipeline of infrared-based facial recognition as it intersects with Automated License Plate Recognition (ALPR) surveillance infrastructure. While ALPR systems and facial recognition systems share common infrared illumination technology and are increasingly deployed in overlapping physical environments, the two remain distinct capture modalities with different operational requirements.

The core finding: facial recognition operates through a five-stage pipeline where each stage introduces measurable error that compounds downstream.

2. ALPR Camera Systems & Infrastructure

System Architecture

ALPR (Automated License Plate Recognition) systems are purpose-built camera arrays designed to capture license plates at highway speeds under any lighting condition. The primary vendors in the United States are Flock Safety, Motorola Solutions (Vigilant), and a tier of municipal procurement systems.

A typical ALPR deployment consists of:

  • Self-illuminating IR cameras with pulsed infrared LEDs synchronized to image capture
  • Fixed-focus optics tuned for targets at 2.4 to 10.4 meters (8-34 ft)
  • IP67-sealed housings rated for continuous outdoor deployment
  • Eye-safety certified illuminators emitting no visible light
  • Edge compute modules performing OCR and vehicle classification on-device
Critical Distinction Current ALPR vendors explicitly disclaim facial recognition capability. Flock Safety states its system "does not recognize faces or identify people." Vigilant/Motorola units are described by deploying agencies as capturing "license plates and vehicles" only. The convergence concern is ecosystem-level data sharing and co-located camera arrays, not integrated face+plate capture in a single unit.

Vehicle Attribute Recognition

Beyond plate OCR, modern ALPR systems classify vehicle attributes: make, color, body type, and distinguishing features (roof racks, bumper stickers, damage patterns). These "vehicle fingerprints" serve as secondary identification when plates are obscured, but they operate on vehicle geometry, not biometric features.

Data Architecture

ALPR data flows into vendor-managed databases: Vigilant LEARN, Motorola's cloud platform, and Flock Safety's proprietary store. Cross-vendor data sharing agreements mean a plate captured by one system may be searchable across multiple jurisdictions. This infrastructure creates the data backbone onto which facial recognition could theoretically be layered, even though no current vendor offers that integration.

3. Infrared Illumination: Physics & Protocol

Wavelength Selection

Near-infrared (NIR) illumination operates in the 750-1400 nm band, invisible to the human eye. The two dominant wavelengths in surveillance applications are:

850 nm Standard

The workhorse wavelength for facial recognition. Slightly visible as a faint red glow from the LED array. Higher quantum efficiency in standard CMOS sensors. Penetrates the epidermis to approximately 2-3 mm depth.

Preferred in European ANPR systems for its balance of sensor efficiency and capture quality.

940 nm Covert

Completely invisible to the human eye. Lower CMOS quantum efficiency requires higher illumination power. Relatively stable against hydration changes in skin. Preferred in US ALPR deployments for covert operation.

Neonatal NIR studies confirm minimal water-absorption variation in the 890-940 nm window.

Illumination Protocol

ALPR and facial recognition IR illuminators use a strobed pulse protocol rather than continuous illumination. The camera sensor sends a synchronization signal to the LED array, triggering IR strobe pulses timed to the exposure window. This approach:

  • Reduces average power consumption and heat generation
  • Allows higher peak illumination intensity during the exposure window
  • Minimizes interference from ambient IR sources (sunlight, other systems)
  • Extends LED lifespan through duty-cycle management

Eye Safety & Range

At wavelengths below 1000 nm, eye-safe power levels limit effective range to approximately 1 meter for face capture. A patent filing notes that at 1250-1600 nm (SWIR), eye-safe radiant power is roughly 100x higher than at 940 nm, extending potential range to approximately 10x. However, SWIR sensors are significantly more expensive and not yet standard in surveillance applications.

Operational Constraint The ~1m effective range for NIR face recognition is a fundamental physics constraint. ALPR systems operate at 2.4-10.4m. This range mismatch is why the two modalities remain separate systems, even when co-located.

4. NIR-Skin Tissue Interaction

Chromophore Absorption in the NIR Band

When near-infrared photons strike skin, three chromophores dominate the optical response:

ChromophorePeak AbsorptionEffect on NIR ImagingDepth Influence
Melanin Broadband, decreasing into NIR Strongly influences NIR intensity and sensitivity. Varies with skin tone (Fitzpatrick I-VI) Epidermis (0-0.1 mm)
Hemoglobin ~760 nm (deoxy), ~940 nm (oxy) Absorption drops sharply entering NIR. Reveals vascular patterns beneath epidermis Dermis (0.1-2 mm)
Water ~970 nm, ~1450 nm Minimal intensity change between 890-940 nm. Dominates at longer wavelengths All layers

The Melanin Problem

Research on neonatal NIR spectroscopy confirmed that melanin volume "strongly influenced NIR intensity and sensitivity" across the 850-940 nm band. This has direct implications for facial recognition accuracy across demographic groups:

  • Higher melanin concentration absorbs more NIR photons, reducing signal-to-noise ratio
  • The effect is not uniform: it varies with wavelength, angle of incidence, and skin hydration
  • Correction requires either independent melanin measurement or computational modeling
  • Below the epidermis, melanin is "practically absent," so sub-epidermal features (vascular patterns) are more consistent across skin tones

Penetration and Scattering

NIR wavelengths penetrate deeper into tissue than visible light, but with increased scattering. Multispectral imaging studies report that wavelengths closer to the infrared "penetrate deeper into skin, which lowers contrast due to higher light scattering." This creates a tradeoff: deeper penetration reveals more sub-surface features but reduces surface contrast that landmark detection algorithms rely on.

Demographic Bias Vector The melanin-NIR interaction is a documented source of performance variation in facial recognition systems. NIST FRVT demographic studies confirm measurably different false match and false non-match rates across demographic groups. The physics of NIR-skin interaction is one root cause.

5. Facial Geometry: Nodal Points & Detection Regions

The 80-Nodal-Point Framework

The facial recognition industry uses the term "nodal points" to describe the measurable landmarks on a human face. The commonly cited figure of ~80 nodal points traces back to Visionics Corporation's FACEIT system and has been propagated through patent filings and vendor documentation.

These points map to specific anatomical features:

Key Measurement Categories

RegionMeasurementsNodal PointsDiscriminative Value
Interpupillary Distance between pupil centers 2 High: primary alignment anchor and scale reference
Eye Sockets Depth, width, inner/outer corners, upper/lower lids 12-16 per eye Very High: bone structure creates unique shadow patterns under IR
Nose Bridge width, tip position, nostril width, length 8-12 High: 3D projection creates strong IR reflectance signature
Cheekbones Prominence, width, height relative to eyes 4-6 Medium-High: structural landmark resistant to expression change
Jawline Width, angle, chin position, contour 10-14 Medium: varies with weight and aging
Mouth Width, lip thickness, corner positions 8-12 Low-Medium: highly variable with expression
Forehead/Brow Height, brow ridge prominence, hairline 6-8 Medium: brow ridge is structural; hairline is unreliable

From Points to Template

The measured distances, angles, and ratios between nodal points are encoded as a numerical "faceprint," described in patent literature as "a string of numbers or a mathematical formula." Modern systems have moved beyond explicit geometric measurement to learned feature extraction (see Section 7), but the nodal point framework remains relevant for:

  • Face alignment prior to embedding (5-point or 68-point landmark detection)
  • Quality assessment (are the key regions visible and properly illuminated?)
  • 3D face mesh construction (Google ML Kit: 468-point mesh)
  • Liveness detection (verifying 3D depth at key nodal points)

Critically, only 14-22 nodal points are reported as necessary for successful recognition in the FACEIT system. The remaining points provide redundancy and improve robustness to partial occlusion.

6. The Periocular Region & Discriminative Features

Why the Eye Region Dominates

Biometric research consistently identifies the periocular region (the area surrounding the eye, including the eyebrow, eyelid, and surrounding skin) as "one of the most discriminative regions of the face." This region is significant because:

  • Bone structure visibility: The orbital bone creates consistent shadow and depth patterns under IR illumination that are resistant to expression changes
  • Texture richness: Fine skin texture, wrinkle patterns, and micro-features in this region are highly individual
  • Robustness to occlusion: When lower face is masked, periocular features alone can achieve usable recognition rates
  • NIR advantage: The periocular area contains "more gender-related information than the iris" in NIR images, suggesting rich sub-surface feature capture

Discriminative Feature Hierarchy

Deep learning attention map studies and occlusion-based analyses reveal a consistent hierarchy of facial regions by discriminative power:

Periocular Recognition Under Challenging Conditions

Research using adaptive spatial transformation networks demonstrates that deep models can learn to automatically crop and focus on the "most discriminative areas in the feature map" of the periocular region. These networks use parallel local branches that each learn a different spatial transformation, then fuse their outputs with a global branch for final classification.

However, periocular recognition in uncontrolled settings remains challenging: illumination variation, occlusion, pose changes, and low resolution "increase within-class variability and between-class similarity," degrading performance significantly from laboratory benchmarks.

7. AI Model Architectures: Margin-Based Loss Functions

The Angular Margin Family

Modern facial recognition is dominated by a family of loss functions that operate on the angular geometry of embedding space. All three major variants, SphereFace, CosFace, and ArcFace, share the same core mechanism: they modify the softmax classification boundary to enforce tighter intra-class clustering and wider inter-class separation on a hypersphere.

ModelLoss FormulationMargin TypeOptimal MarginLFW Acc.YTF Acc.
SphereFace 2017 Multiplicative angular: cos(m * θ) Nonlinear angular m = 1.35 99.42% 95.0%
CosFace 2018 Additive cosine: cos(θ) - m Nonlinear angular m = 0.35 99.73% 97.6%
ArcFace 2019 Additive angular: cos(θ + m) Constant linear angular m = 0.50 99.83% 98.02%
FaceNet 2015 Triplet loss with Euclidean distance Distance margin varies 99.65% 95.1%
Benchmark Caveat The LFW scores above are not directly comparable. Training data differs dramatically: SphereFace used 0.5M images, CosFace 5M, ArcFace trained on MS1MV3 with ResNet100. Independent controlled comparisons (ExpFace, 2025) show all three angular margins "perform similarly in general" when training conditions are held constant.

How ArcFace Creates Correlations

ArcFace's training objective illustrates how all margin-based models create correlations between facial features:

  1. Feature normalization: Both the feature vector x and the classification weight vector W are L2-normalized, projecting them onto a unit hypersphere
  2. Angular measurement: The dot product between normalized vectors equals the cosine of the angle between them: WTx = cos(θ)
  3. Margin injection: An additive angular margin m is added to the angle for the correct class: cos(θ + m). This makes the decision boundary stricter for the correct identity
  4. Geodesic correspondence: Because the margin operates directly on the angle, it corresponds exactly to geodesic distance on the hypersphere, providing a "better geometric attribute" than multiplicative or cosine margins

The result: each weight vector Wi in the final classification layer becomes a class center for identity i on the hypersphere. During inference, these weights are discarded and only the feature extraction backbone is kept. The embedding network has learned to map faces of the same person to nearby regions on the hypersphere, and faces of different people to distant regions.

8. The Embedding Pipeline: Detection to Vector

Stage-by-Stage Pipeline

Every facial recognition system, regardless of its model architecture, follows a standardized pipeline from raw image to match decision:

Stage 1: Face Detection

The detector locates face bounding boxes and facial landmarks in the input frame.

DetectorArchitectureLandmarksSpeedAccuracy
MTCNN 3-stage cascade (P-Net, R-Net, O-Net) with NMS 5 points ~30 fps Standard baseline
RetinaFace Single-stage with Feature Pyramid Network 5 points ~15-25 fps More stable boxes and landmarks
dlib HOG/CNN HOG + SVM or CNN detector 68 points ~5-15 fps Dense landmark model

The five standard landmarks from MTCNN/RetinaFace are: left eye center, right eye center, nose tip, left mouth corner, right mouth corner. These five points are sufficient for the alignment step.

Stage 2: Face Alignment

A similarity transformation (rotation, scale, translation) is computed from the five detected landmarks to a canonical reference position. The goal is to normalize pose so that both eyes are horizontal and the face occupies a consistent region of the crop.

The transformation is applied to the original image, then a tight crop is extracted. Research indicates that alignment should precede cropping: "cropping before rotating removes border texture," which degrades embedding quality.

Stage 3: Preprocessing

The aligned face crop is resized to the model's expected input dimensions and pixel values are normalized:

  • Resolution: 112 x 112 pixels (ArcFace convention) or 160x160 (FaceNet)
  • Normalization: (pixel - 127.5) / 128, mapping 8-bit values to approximately [-1, +1]
  • Color: RGB input preserved (not converted to grayscale)

Stage 4: Feature Extraction (Embedding)

The CNN backbone processes the normalized crop and outputs a fixed-length feature vector:

  • Typical output: 512 floating-point values (ArcFace, CosFace)
  • The vector is L2-normalized to unit length, placing it on a hypersphere
  • This vector IS the biometric template, not the image itself

Stage 5: Comparison & Decision

Two embedding vectors are compared using a distance metric:

Cosine Similarity

Measures the angle between vectors, ignoring magnitude. Values near 1.0 indicate high similarity. Used by ArcFace and most modern systems.

Typical threshold: 0.3-0.4 cosine distance

Euclidean Distance

Measures straight-line distance in embedding space. Lower values indicate greater similarity. Used by FaceNet's triplet loss formulation.

On normalized vectors, equivalent to cosine distance

The match decision is binary: if the distance is below a threshold T, the system declares a match. The choice of T determines the operating point on the false match rate vs. false non-match rate curve.

9. Quality & Accuracy Scoring: NIST FRVT & ISO 29794-5

What a Quality Score Means

A face image quality score is a prediction of how well a given image will perform when submitted to a recognition algorithm. Per ISO/IEC 2382-37 and the NIST FRVT definition, a quality score "should reflect face recognition performance," not aesthetic quality or resolution alone.

ISO/IEC 29794-5:2025 (first edition, published April 2025) defines two types of quality measures:

Unified Quality Score

A single integer from 0 to 100 (higher is better) that predicts overall recognition utility. This is the primary gatekeeping value: images below a threshold are rejected for re-capture.

Component Scores

Individual measures for specific quality aspects: background uniformity, illumination uniformity, eyes open, mouth closed, single face present, pose deviation, natural color.

How Quality Scores Are Computed

Quality assessment algorithms fall into two categories:

Rule-Based (Component Measures)

Traditional approaches compute each component independently:

  • Background uniformity: Variance of pixel values outside the face bounding box
  • Illumination uniformity: Ratio of luminance between left and right face halves
  • Pose estimation: Yaw, pitch, and roll angles from landmark geometry
  • Eyes open / Mouth closed: Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR) and Mouth Aspect Ratio (MAR) from landmarks
  • Sharpness: Laplacian variance or high-frequency energy of the face crop

Learned (Unified Score)

Modern FIQA algorithms train a neural network to predict recognition utility directly:

  • CR-FIQA learns quality from "sample relative classifiability," measuring how easily a recognition model can correctly classify the image relative to other samples of the same identity
  • CNN-based approaches train on four degradation factors (alignment, occlusion, pose, blur) and fuse component scores into a unified prediction
  • OFIQ (Open Source Face Image Quality) is the BSI-developed reference implementation of ISO 29794-5, providing both unified and component scores as an open-source C++ library (MIT license)

NIST FRVT / FATE Quality Evaluation

NIST's Face Analysis Technology Evaluation (FATE) Quality track is the authoritative benchmark for FIQA algorithms. It operates as follows:

  1. Submission: Vendors submit FIQA algorithms that output quality scores for face images
  2. Evaluation dataset: NIST runs the algorithm against multiple face recognition systems and dataset types
  3. Error-versus-reject curves: For each quality threshold, NIST measures the recognition error rate on images that pass the threshold. The curve shows how aggressively rejecting low-quality images improves recognition accuracy
  4. Cross-system prediction: A good FIQA algorithm should predict performance across multiple recognition systems, not just one

FRVT Scoring Methodology

The FRVT recognition evaluation itself uses the following scoring approach:

MetricDefinitionTypical Target
False Match Rate (FMR) Proportion of impostor comparisons scoring above threshold T 10-6 (high security)
False Non-Match Rate (FNMR) Proportion of genuine comparisons scoring below threshold T < 0.01 at target FMR
False Positive Identification Rate (FPIR) Rate of false alarms in a 1:N search against a gallery 0.0003 (1 in ~3,333)
True Positive Identification Rate (TPIR) Rate of correct identifications in a 1:N search > 0.99 at target FPIR

Threshold construction: NIST builds candidate thresholds from quantiles of the observed impostor score distribution, spaced evenly on a logarithmic scale across the target FMR range. A deployer then selects the threshold that achieves their desired security level.

Demographic equity: NISTIR 8429 notes that a global threshold can be raised to hold the FMR of the worst-performing demographic group at target, which reduces false matches for all other groups as well, though at the cost of increased false non-matches across the board.

The scoring system operates as a two-tier gate:

  1. Quality gate (ISO 29794-5): Component scores check capture conditions (background, illumination, pose, eyes/mouth state). The unified score (0-100) predicts recognition utility. Images below threshold are rejected for recapture. This gate operates BEFORE any identity comparison.
  2. Match gate (FRVT): Accepted images are embedded and compared. The similarity score is checked against a threshold T set to achieve a target false match rate. Scores above T are declared matches.

Key Findings

Finding 1: ALPR and Facial Recognition Remain Separate Systems Despite sharing NIR illumination technology, ALPR operates at 2.4-10.4m range while NIR facial recognition is limited to ~1m at eye-safe power levels. No current vendor offers integrated plate+face capture. The convergence risk is at the data infrastructure and deployment location level, not the sensor level.
Finding 2: The Periocular Region Is the Primary Identity Signal Bone-defined orbital structure, eye socket depth, and periocular skin texture are consistently the most discriminative facial features. The lower face (mouth, jawline) contributes less to identity and is more variable across expression and aging.
Finding 3: Model Architecture Matters Less Than Training Data Under controlled conditions, ArcFace, CosFace, and SphereFace achieve comparable accuracy. The dominant variable is training dataset size and quality, not the margin formulation. ArcFace's geometric advantage is theoretical elegance, not measurable performance lift.
Finding 4: Quality Scoring Predicts Accuracy, But Is Matcher-Dependent A FIQA algorithm's utility depends on the recognition system it is paired with. The ISO 29794-5 standard and NIST FATE evaluation address this through cross-system evaluation, but no single quality score universally predicts performance across all matchers.
Finding 5: NIR-Melanin Interaction Is a Documented Bias Vector Melanin volume "strongly influences NIR intensity and sensitivity" in the 850-940 nm band. This physical effect compounds with training data bias and threshold selection to produce measurable demographic performance variation documented in NIST FRVT demographic studies.
